# Oil-Palm Operations: Lifecycle and Simulation Reference

**Status:** research/design reference (sources checked 1 October 2026). It is not an operating manual, legal opinion, agronomic recommendation, or claim that the current prototype implements this lifecycle.

**Purpose:** give the game a source-backed end-to-end process model and a shared vocabulary for simulation, UI, art, and acceptance work. The target is a bounded strategy view of one oil-palm estate, four managed blocks, a limited crew roster, and one local mill/outlet. **The calendar is accelerated; the real operational stages, causal order, material flows, and dependencies are not optional.** Repetitive work may be batch-resolved at block/cohort scale, but the simulation must still represent each operation and its inputs, outputs, capacity, costs, and consequences.

## Process at a glance

```text
Screen site, tenure/rights, soil, water and safeguards
    ├── secure suitable planting material → pre-nursery → main nursery ─┐
    └── map blocks, access, drainage and planting layout ───────────────┤
                                                                        ↓
Protect soil / establish ground cover → prepare planting points → field planting
    → immature-palm care, gap filling and monitoring
    → flowering and pollination → bunch development and ripening
    → repeated harvest rounds + pruning/frond stacking + loose-fruit recovery
    → field collection point, records, grading/weighing and transport
    → mill intake → sterilise → thresh → digest → press
       ├── crude-oil clarification/drying/storage → local sale/outlet
       ├── fibre/nut separation → kernel recovery/drying/storage → kernel outlet
       ├── fibre/shell → boiler/energy where the selected mill uses that route
       ├── empty fruit bunches/fronds → mulch/compost/other approved route
       └── POME and other wastewater → treatment, monitoring and permitted reuse/discharge
    → assess declining stand and replant selected blocks in a planned rotation
```

Nursery preparation and site planning can overlap. A field palm cannot be planted before suitable planting material and its planting site are ready; harvest cannot precede maturity/ripening; FFB cannot be milled before delivery; mill outputs and wastes cannot exist before processing. Replanting restarts the nursery/establishment chain and temporarily reduces the estate's producing area.

## Lifecycle and operation sequence

| Stage | Typical real-world work and why it matters | Game-scale dependency and visual read |
|---|---|---|
| **0. Site, rights and safeguards** | Establish the legal/operational boundary and land-use history; assess soil, slope, drainage, water access, roads, environmental sensitivities and affected communities before development. Plan protected areas, riparian buffers, no-go areas and worker/community protections for the chosen jurisdiction/scenario. This is not a universal instruction to clear forest or drain wetlands. [1][12] | The player first sees a suitability/constraint map and block boundaries. A development action must be limited to a scenario-approved area; habitat and water buffers remain visible and affect choices. A fictional scenario rule must be labelled as such. |
| **1. Planting material and nursery** | Commercial seed/seedling quality strongly affects the life of a long-lived stand. A common nursery pathway uses a small early-stage bag/bed followed by a larger main-nursery bag; both stages need appropriate water, growing medium, weed/pest checks, nutrition, selection/culling and careful handling. Estates may raise seedlings themselves or buy them from a specialist nursery. [1][2][3] | Represent the source of seedlings explicitly: run a nursery or buy a finite planting-material order. Preserve the sequence `early seedling → selected nursery seedling → field-ready seedling`; nursery work and site planning may progress in parallel. Show rejects/shortages and water/crew needs rather than spawning mature palms. |
| **2. Field establishment** | Map the block and planting layout; prepare planting points and access; retain soil cover and manage runoff/erosion; establish suitable ground cover where used; transplant healthy nursery stock at a locally suitable time and depth. Spacing, planting pattern, drainage and ground preparation depend on site, soil, terrain and local practice. [1][3][12] | Planting consumes ready seedlings, prepared positions and crew time. The block changes from prepared ground to planted ground. Keep protected features, roads and water structures spatially coherent; do not use one universal spacing or drainage design as a global fact. |
| **3. Immature stand (pre-bearing years)** | Establishment care includes survival checks and replacement of missing/deformed plants; weed and access-path management; cover/mulch and soil-water care; appropriately monitored nutrition; and pest/disease scouting. Some local systems use early inflorescence removal, but the duration and practice require local agronomic review. There is no commercial harvest simply because the game calendar advanced one short session. [3][8] | Cohorts remain non-bearing until their reviewed scenario age and conditions permit fruiting. Maintenance tasks have labour/input costs and improve access or reduce risk; no early commercial FFB. A compressed calendar can jump between reviewed months/seasons while retaining this multi-year lead. |
| **4. Reproductive development and mature-palm care** | Oil palm has separate male and female inflorescences on the same plant, normally at different times; pollination and fruit set lead to a developing bunch. A bunch commonly develops over roughly five to six months after pollination, with variation. Mature stands still need field access, suitable frond management, nutrition/soil-water care, road/drain maintenance and scouting. Excessive pruning can reduce productive leaf area. [3][4][5][8] | The crop model should distinguish age/cohort, health/condition, developing fruit, ripe fruit and available harvest work. Not every palm or bunch ripens together. The player sees repeated rounds, not one annual “harvest all” switch. Pollination can be an aggregate success/risk factor at launch rather than a detailed insect population model. [5] |
| **5. Pest, disease and soil/water management** | Scout and record symptoms, then select locally appropriate responses. Examples reported in Southeast Asian smallholder guidance include rats, rhinoceros beetles (*Oryctes*), leaf-eating caterpillars and *Ganoderma* stem rot; other pests/diseases vary by location. Integrated approaches can include sanitation, monitoring, habitat for beneficial organisms, biological controls and registered chemical controls where needed. Soil cover, drainage, water quality and nutrient recycling also matter. [9][12] | A risk icon or health tint is a warning, not a species diagnosis. A task should follow observation/threshold logic and consume the appropriate crew/tool/input. Do not show chemical mixing or doses as advice; use generic approved-input/PPE visuals and a “follow local label/training” rule. Risk and treatment effects remain scenario parameters until reviewed. |
| **6. Harvest rounds** | Harvest teams revisit blocks repeatedly. Readiness is judged using a locally defined standard; fruit colour and detached/loose fruit are common cues, but a fixed loose-fruit count is not universal. Workers cut ripe bunches with a suitable tool for palm height, avoid damaging the palm, manage necessary fronds, and collect loose fruits (“brondolan”). Bunch preparation/stalk trimming and recordkeeping may be part of the local grading system. [6][7] | A ripe bunch becomes a harvest job only when the block round, trained crew and tool are available. Show cut FFB, collected loose fruit and any missed crop as distinct states. Tool choice can be simplified to short-handled **dodos** for accessible palms and long-pole **egrek** for taller palms; exact practice should be locally checked. |
| **7. Field collection, grading and transport** | Bunches and loose fruits are gathered from the palm base and moved to an in-field/roadside collection point. Depending on terrain and scale, this may use baskets, carts, a motorbike/trailer, tractor/implement or manual carrying. Batches are counted/recorded, graded/weighed at the appropriate point, and linked to their block/supplier. Road condition, labour, collection capacity and careful handling influence losses and quality. Rapid delivery is commonly targeted; a 24-hour guideline appears in some smallholder manuals, not as a universal legal rule. [6][7] | Harvest, loose-fruit collection, collection-point capacity, vehicle capacity, road access, mill intake capacity and time since cutting are separate states. A full collection point or closed road blocks further evacuation; delay/damage changes the scenario quality outcome. Keep quantities conserved from field to intake. |
| **8. Mill intake and processing** | A representative industrial sequence is **weigh/receive and inspect FFB → sterilise with steam → thresh fruits from the bunch → digest/pound fruit → press mesocarp for crude palm oil → screen/clarify and dry oil → store CPO**. The exact configuration is mill-specific; this order is one representative example, not a universal plant blueprint. [10][14] The parallel kernel route separates fibre and nuts; nuts are dried/cracked/separated to recover kernels, which commonly go to a separate kernel-crushing plant for palm-kernel oil. Exact machine layout and degree of integration depend on mill size and technology. [10] | The launch outlet is one bounded local mill. Use a small number of readable station modules that preserve the chain and throughput constraints; the player need not operate every valve. FFB intake, batches in process, CPO, kernels and residuals should be separate inventories. The launch ends at crude products/local outlet; **no refinery or broad downstream product chain** is implied. |
| **9. By-products, environment and business records** | Mill side streams include empty fruit bunches (EFB), mesocarp fibre, shells, press residues and palm-oil-mill effluent (POME). Depending on mill design and local rules, fibre/shell can supply boiler energy, EFB/fronds may be recycled as mulch/compost, kernels may be stored/sold, and POME requires treatment and monitoring before permitted reuse or discharge. One conventional pond route uses cooling/mixing followed by anaerobic treatment and facultative/aerobic polishing; some sites capture biogas, while other treatment technologies are also used. This is an example pathway, not a universal plant layout. Commercial records link block/supplier, weight/grade, transport, mill batch, products, payments, inputs, labour and relevant environmental/safety records. [10][11][12][14] | A by-product cannot disappear without a selected use/disposal route. One local mill can use a simplified, explicit treatment pathway (for example a pond-system or captured-biogas scenario); the map and UI should show what route is modelled. Traceability and worker/environment safeguards are decision systems, not only decorative props. |
| **10. Replanting and the next rotation** | Replant decisions depend on the stand's yield, height/harvestability, disease, establishment cost, prices, labour and long-term plan—not a universal age threshold. Replanting is commonly phased by block to manage investment and avoid a sudden loss of FFB supply. Economic timing depends on prices, costs and yield profile rather than a universal age. [15] Planning includes the next planting-material order, suitable land/site preparation, safe removal/sanitation of the old stand, residue handling and re-establishment. | Older/declining palms lead to a visible replant choice. Replanting one block resets its cohort and requires the nursery/site-prep/planting dependencies again; it also creates a multi-year production gap and lowers feedstock available to the mill. Keep the four-block estate finite—no automatic new land or infinite expansion. Whether all replant actions ship at launch remains governed by the roadmap/milestone gate. |

## Labour, safety and operating capacity are cross-cutting

Real operations are planned around task timing, travel, skill, equipment and available crew—not just a worker counter. Nursery care, field maintenance/scouting, fertilizer or other input handling, pruning/harvest, loose-fruit recovery, loading/transport, mill operations and recordkeeping require different tools and training. The game may bundle people into a limited number of crews, but should make workload, queueing, bottlenecks, PPE and training legible. Injury, emergency response and welfare are not decorative; the chosen scenario needs rules appropriate to its jurisdiction and scale. [12]

RSPO's 2024 Principles & Criteria cover legal operation, productivity and traceability, communities/human rights, worker conditions, and ecosystem stewardship. RSPO is a voluntary standard, not a replacement for applicable law. If the campaign is specifically set in Indonesia, the current legal reference is Perpres No. 16 Tahun 2025 on the Indonesian Sustainable Palm Oil certification system (in force from 19 March 2025, replacing Perpres 44/2020); implementation detail must be checked against current subordinate rules and the chosen business type. [13] The project has not established a legal-compliance simulation scope.

## Time compression: what may and may not change

**May accelerate:** the displayed calendar and animation/travel duration. The game can aggregate equivalent palms and workers into cohorts/crews so it remains playable, but aggregation is a resolution shortcut—not permission to omit operations.

**Must preserve:** every stage in the lifecycle table; prerequisite and material flow; the multi-year immature period and post-pollination bunch development; soil/water and pest/disease monitoring; harvest rounds, bunch cutting and separate loose-fruit recovery; collection, grading/records and transport; each mill unit operation in order; product/by-product destinations and POME treatment; and the production gap caused by replanting. Site planning and nursery preparation may overlap where the scenario allows; independent block/batch work may run in parallel when labour and capacity permit. Fast-forward changes elapsed time only: it cannot create ready fruit, delivered FFB, processed output or a replanted stand by skipping the intervening stages. Each operation should resolve with explicit states, quantities, inputs, labour/capacity, costs and consequences even when many palms are processed as one cohort.

The native crop model currently covers only part of this chain. Its values and missing processes are documented separately in [`CROP_MODEL.md`](CROP_MODEL.md). This reference does not claim the current prototype implements the operations above or change any existing acceptance status.

## Parameter cautions and regional assumptions

- **Nursery ages, plant density, fertilizer rates, weed-control methods, drainage, harvest interval, ripeness threshold, yield curve and replant age are context-dependent.** The Indonesian 2014 good-cultivation guide, FAO materials, and WUR/SNV smallholder handbook are useful process references, but they do not define one current universal schedule. Treat numeric values as sourced ranges or explicit game-scenario assumptions and seek local agronomic review before using them as factual claims.
- Nursery timing is not a single universal age: legacy references give examples ranging from a few months in a small pre-nursery container, followed by roughly 6–12 months in a field nursery, to transplanting stock at approximately 10–18 months. These are source-specific examples, not a prescribed schedule. [3][4]
- Published descriptions place first fruiting/harvest several years after field planting (often roughly 30–48 months, depending on material, site and what is counted); a particular bunch then takes months to develop after pollination. [3][4][5] Do not equate those two clocks.
- A 24-hour FFB delivery target is useful in some manuals because delay and bruising can affect quality, but local standards, mill arrangements and accepted delivery windows differ. [6][7]
- Harvest round examples are context-specific. In one 2002 Sabah plantation reassessment, an estimated 10-day interval produced 33.7 t/ha of FFB versus 31.6 t/ha at 15 days (about 6.6% higher) in the study plots. Do not transfer that result into a universal interval or yield bonus; labour availability, crop load, ripeness rules and site conditions matter. [7]
- Better-management practices do not guarantee short-term yield or profit gains. A multi-year study of 14 smallholder plantations in Jambi and West Kalimantan reported improvements in some bunch/leaf and nutrient measures, but no significant yield increase during its implementation period. Its overall cost-benefit assessment found that additional fertilizer costs outweighed yield returns, while margins varied by location and year. Treat this as a bounded study result, not a universal forecast. [8]
- A particular palm age such as 25 years is not an objective universal replant trigger. Published economic work demonstrates dependence on FFB price, establishment/maintenance costs, yield profile and discounting; local conditions change the answer. [15]
- Do not lift historical pesticide products/doses, burning practices, spacings or fertilizer tables from older references. Use the current local legal/technical source and qualified advice for any regional rule. FAO's legacy *Modern Oil Palm Cultivation* guide [3] is used only for broad lifecycle sequence, not as authority for present-day land-clearing, safety or chemical practice.
- The user/location context does not itself establish the campaign's province, soil type, mill configuration or legal scenario. Keep those assumptions explicit until the game setting is decided.

## Visual vocabulary for the strategy camera

The minimum readable biological set is not four ornamental palms. It is an *Elaeis guineensis* life-stage family (nursery seedling, field juvenile/immature, young producing, mature producing, and tall/declining), plus a fruit-bunch maturity set and loose-fruit cue. At strategy zoom, representative fronds, fruit colour/position, trunk silhouette and distinct text/pattern overlays matter more than photo detail. Avoid presenting generic coconuts or round clumps of orange spheres as verified oil-palm botany.

Other process cues should be visible at map scale: nursery rows and water supply; planting layout and cover crop; soil/ground/water condition; worker roles, harvest tools and PPE; a distinct roadside collection point and vehicle route; mill intake and a small set of process stations; storage and waste-treatment zones; and clear protected-area, riparian, risk and replanting overlays. Detailed individual inflorescences, pest species, machine internals or lab instruments are close-up/teaching assets, not a prerequisite for a readable strategy map.
